Who Gets to Say What Change Means

Darish K. Still · November 2026

I know change is possible because I've lived it. That's exactly why I have to be careful with it.

When I wrote my positionality statement, I said I don't have a physical disability. This fall I learned the law draws that circle wider than I did. The ADA covers conditions that come and go or are in remission, including mental health conditions. It can also protect people in treatment or recovery from substance use disorder. I'm in long-term recovery. So the honest answer to whether disability touches my life is more complicated than the one I'd been giving.

I still hold a lot of ability privilege. Buildings, forms, and meeting times are built for a body like mine. But I'm paying closer attention now to who gets seen as disabled, and who gets seen as something else.

One story stuck with me. A veteran with post-traumatic stress drank and smoked to stay calm. Employers seemed turned off by him, and the system mostly saw the substance use. In SUD case management, that's a familiar pattern. Someone treats a pain nobody else is treating, and then the only thing anyone writes down is the drug. I spent more than a decade inside systems that treated one problem at a time, so I recognize it.

Words do some of that work. Police read some neurodivergent behavior as "non-compliant." My field uses the same word for clients. I'm going to check my own notes for it.

The piece that hit closest came from a writer named Moore. She said she'd still take a cure if one were offered. She also said chasing a cure could mean missing her life, and the person she'd become. Recovery built a person in me I'd never met before, so I understand why she weighs that cost.

She also wrote about believing she should be able to overcome her disability if she just tried hard enough. That one matters to me as a practitioner. I could pass that belief on to clients without ever saying it out loud, just by treating my recovery as the template for theirs.

So here's the line I hold. My story tells me change is possible. It doesn't tell me what change has to look like for anyone else. The client gets to define that.

In practice, that means asking at intake and at every plan review how someone learns, what access they need, and who already shows up for them. Some of the best care I've read about isn't charity at all. It's people in harm reduction networks handing each other naloxone and test strips. It's solidarity from the people closest to the problem.
