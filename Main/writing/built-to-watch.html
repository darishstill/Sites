Writing · Race

Built to Watch

Darish K. Still · November 2026

I was warned early, and plainly, that white men would work to destroy me. That warning shaped how I learned to move through the world.

I grew up on the crime coverage and War on Drugs imagery of that era. The Central Park Five case was on the news while I was still a kid. That warning landed on top of all of it. What it built in me, I now call racial threat surveillance. I scanned white spaces for danger. I adjusted my voice at work. I felt the exhaustion that comes from doing that for years without a break.

W.E.B. Du Bois had a name for this more than a century ago: double consciousness. I once wrote that I never developed a stable identity, because my sense of self was always organized around managing how other people saw me. It wasn't organized around what was actually there.

For most of my life I treated that as something personal and a little strange. This fall I finally got to set it inside a system. One reading traced racism in this country as one continuous project, from 1492 through slavery, Jim Crow, and mass incarceration. Read that way, the War on Drugs I watched as a kid wasn't a response to a drug problem. It was the same old social control in a new uniform. Beverly Daniel Tatum compares cultural racism to smog. Everyone breathes it, whether they polluted the air or not.

Then I read Ta-Nehisi Coates, and he wouldn't let me keep it abstract. He writes about racism as something that lands on the body. That sent me back to my own word. I'd been describing surveillance as a psychological cost, something in my head. It was never only in my head. It was my body getting ready for something that could actually happen to it. That's what the warning was meant to do.

Here's the hard part. People who are targeted by a system can still absorb its logic and help it run. I have to keep checking myself for that. A case manager who skips that step can end up repeating the exact harm he's supposed to guard against.

I also can't assume my experience maps onto my clients'. The courts, treatment programs, insurers, and employers they deal with often judge people by intent instead of impact. They spread burden and benefit unevenly by race. I'm certain that's unjust. But my job in the room isn't my certainty. It's curiosity about how this person, in this chair, actually lives it. I bring my own watchfulness into the room as something I'm aware of. I don't hand it to a client who never said it was there.
