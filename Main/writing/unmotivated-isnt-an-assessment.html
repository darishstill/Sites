Unmotivated Isn't an Assessment

Darish K. Still · November 2026

When a client misses a session, the easiest word to write in the chart is "unmotivated." I've learned not to trust it.

I grew up in the Philadelphia area, in a family shaped by addiction and poverty. I watched a lack of resources box us in. None of it had anything to do with how hard anyone worked or what kind of people we were.

There's a story this country tells about effort. Work hard enough and you'll be rewarded. If you're poor, you must not have tried. People who grow up without money tend to see through that story early, because their own lives show them how advantage stacks on itself. I saw through it as a kid. This semester gave me the history behind it.

The Black and white wealth gap didn't happen by accident. Between 1933 and 1978, federal programs helped more than thirty-five million families build equity in their homes. At the same time, federal agents ranked Black and mixed neighborhoods as the least desirable, and loan officers lent by those rankings. Today about 44% of Black households own their homes, compared with about 75% of white households. That gap was built on purpose, one generation at a time.

Coming back to college as an adult put me inside a class system again. Some low-income students go hungry over spring break because the dining halls close. Colleges give admission preference to the children of alumni, which makes the door harder for first-generation students like me. I've also felt the strange confusion some working-class people describe after they earn a degree. You look at the letters after your name and you're not sure they're yours.

I have to be honest about the other side, too. My years in leadership taught me how institutions work, and many of my clients never got that knowledge. That's an advantage. It isn't proof that anyone could do what I did.

Class shows up in the chart. A hospital can keep a Medicaid patient fewer days than a privately insured patient with the same condition. And a word like "unmotivated" can land in a treatment record when the real reason a client missed was no bus fare or no child care.

Effort matters to me. It helped me rebuild my life. But effort is never the whole story. So when a client misses a session, I've committed to asking about transportation, money, and child care first. Then I'll write down what I actually learned, not a label.
