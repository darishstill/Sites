A Late Bloomer on Both Sides of the Room

Darish K. Still · November 2026

I came back to school in my forties. I call myself a late bloomer, and I've learned that age puts me on both sides of a line.

In the job market, I'm close to the age where discrimination tends to start. AARP research finds it often begins as early as 50, and I'm in my late forties. People over 40 already run into it.

The classroom is the other side. Most of my classmates are traditional college age, and there I'm the one with status. Younger adults often get treated as inexperienced. They get left out of decisions that affect them, and they defer to older people even when they know more. When I speak first in a group, my age can quiet a younger classmate who actually knows more than I do. So I try to notice that before I treat my experience as authority.

Age doesn't land on everyone the same way. One study of older adults in Massachusetts found that Black elders live with more chronic disease than white elders. Most of that gap shrank once the researchers accounted for money, education, neighborhood, work, and access to care. Three out of four older Black adults living alone there had incomes below what it costs to meet basic needs. For white elders it was 58%. Health in old age is the bill for a lifetime of unequal conditions. As a Black man, I see my own future in those numbers.

In treatment, ageism can hide things. Health care often writes off depression and memory changes as just getting old, which leads to misdiagnosis and too much medication. In SUD work, that same assumption can hide a substance use disorder in an older client, because nobody expects to find one. It can also run the other way. An older client gets written off as too set in their ways to change. Most elders keep the ability to learn and remember. One writer asks us to assume elders are potentially brilliant, with long futures ahead of them.

I believe reinvention has no age limit, because I'm living it. But I have to make room for something my own story doesn't cover. An older client might want rest, not reinvention, and that's their call. So I'm committing to screen every client for substance use at intake, whatever their age. I'll ask older clients how they like to learn and communicate instead of guessing what technology they can use. And I'll ask them what they already know about their own recovery.
