Growth Isn't a Straight Line

Darish K. Still · November 2026

Going back over old ground isn't failure. I didn't always believe that.

This fall I read two ways of picturing how people grow in their understanding of who they are. The first one is simple. You step out of your comfort zone toward a learning edge, and what feels familiar gets a little wider each time. It's a good picture of one hard moment. But every arrow in it points outward. Nothing in it points back.

The second picture comes from Rita Hardiman and Bailey Jackson. They describe stages people move through as they wake up to how the world is arranged. Then they add one word that changed how I read my own life: recycling. A new relationship, a new experience, or a new piece of truth can send you back to an earlier stage. Their model expects that. It doesn't call it a defect.

That's how my own understanding of race and class actually grew. I'd move forward and feel like I finally had something figured out. Then I'd run into a person or a fact that unsettled it, and I'd be back asking questions I thought I'd already answered. Each time I came back, I went deeper. Nothing I'd learned got erased.

If all you have is the outward arrow, you've only got one word for that experience. You call it backsliding. And backsliding is a word that tells people they've disqualified themselves.

In recovery work, that word can do real damage. It tells someone a setback means they're done, when it might be the moment they go deeper. I don't think that's honest. People rarely arrive at insight in a straight line, and the people trained to help them don't either.

So I'm holding onto the recycling idea, for my clients and for myself. When someone comes back around to an old question, I want to treat it as the work continuing, not the work collapsing. Stabilize first. Then go deeper. Then expect to come back around again.
