Grace and the Default

Darish K. Still · November 2026

I'm a Christian, and my faith is the default almost everywhere I go. Taking that seriously means taking a hard look at how I lead.

When I was a boy in church, I heard a pastor say gay people go to hell. The congregation said amen. Nobody laid a hand on me. I absorbed the verdict anyway and carried it for years. My faith today is rooted in grace, not fear. But I didn't get here by pretending that sermon never happened.

That's the strange place I stand. I was a target inside my own faith. That same faith still hands me privileges I never asked for. My holidays are already on the school and work calendar. My language of grace and calling passes without comment. Nobody reads my faith as a threat. And one of the quietest privileges of all is being able to say I don't have any.

There's another layer. As a Black man, I come from people who were enslaved with scripture as the excuse, carried on ships with names like Jesus and Grace of God. The faith of the dominant culture is also the faith my ancestors reshaped to survive and resist. The colonists read the Exodus story and saw themselves as Israel and King George as Pharaoh, while slavery was legal in every colony. The enslaved read the same story and saw Pharaoh in their enslavers. Frederick Douglass named the split in 1845. He separated "the Christianity of Christ" from "the slaveholding religion of this land." Sorting those two apart in my own faith is work that's mine to do.

It matters in my work every day. Twelve-step recovery asks people to turn their will over to God "as we understood Him." For a Muslim, Jewish, Native, or atheist client, or someone who's been hurt by church, it can sound like one more room built for someone else. Three federal appeals courts have ruled that forcing people into 12-step meetings through prison, probation, or parole violates the separation of church and state. So for justice-involved clients, I have to confirm that a secular option was actually offered.

Then there's my own leadership. My faith runs through how I communicate with the people I supervise, sometimes in small ways I've stopped noticing. Interns can't easily push back on their supervisor. A Muslim or atheist intern will probably never tell me that something I say or write bothers them. That silence is how a default keeps itself in place.

My faith is personal, and I'm keeping it. How I express it from a position of authority is a professional decision. Every client I serve has the right to worship, or not to, free of my beliefs. I protect their practice, and I keep mine out of the room. I'd rather catch my own defaults before someone with less power has to point them out.
