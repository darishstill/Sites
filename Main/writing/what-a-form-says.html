What a Form Says Before You Do

Darish K. Still · November 2026

For years I hid my sexual orientation at work, including in clinical settings. I'd decided it wasn't safe to tell the truth. So I know how people read a room before they say a word.

One list of everyday heterosexual privileges named things I used to weigh. Straight people don't worry about what happens if co-workers find out. They mention a partner at work and nobody notices. They never have to lie about their weekend. The quiet assumption that everyone is straight unless proven otherwise sorts a room before anyone speaks.

The same list showed me where I hold power. One item says a straight person can simply choose not to look at any of this. I have choices like that too. As a man, I can overlook sexism. As a Christian, I can overlook how my faith is treated as the norm. And church life is part of this. Straight couples walk in expecting a welcome and a blessing. I live inside that tension in my own faith.

This matters in practice, because clients read a room the way I did. Picture an intake form that assumes every spouse is the other gender. It tells a client what's safe to say before they ever open their mouth. Some providers can refuse treatment on religious grounds. And there's a hard argument I read this fall about juvenile justice. A system built around treatment can still teach queer kids they're wrong and need fixing, and that falls hardest on low-income kids of color. I work in treatment. I have to ask what my own forms and silences teach.

It shows up in small moments, too. Picture a break room. One person says "that's so gay," one person objects, and everybody else stays quiet. Researchers who study kids say those insults keep inequality in place. Adults in a break room do the same work.

My faith anchors my belief that every person has inherent dignity. Romans 8 tells me nothing can separate us from God's love. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights says it plainly, too: "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." My job asks what I actually do with that belief once I'm at work.

I've also learned not to call myself an ally and rest there. A sticker or a statement can stand in for action. The people I support decide whether my work counts as solidarity. So when a colleague says "that's so gay," I'll answer plainly in the moment and follow up privately afterward. And I'll keep checking the forms I use for language that assumes who someone's spouse is.
