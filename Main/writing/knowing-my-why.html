Knowing My Why

Darish K. Still · November 2026

My masculinity has been judged from two directions. Neither one gives me the right to speak for women.

In fourth grade, some boys decided the way I moved and talked meant I was gay. So I deepened my voice and stiffened my walk. Years later, job interviewers heard some of that same refinement and called me "articulate" and "well-spoken." Men of color get measured against an unstated white, straight ideal. Depending on the room, we're too masculine or not masculine enough. And calling individual men "toxic" doesn't touch the structure that keeps producing the judgment.

Being on the receiving end of those norms doesn't take me out of the conversation about men. Jackson Katz argues that violence against women is a men's issue, since more than 99% of rape is committed by men. He also ties violence between men, down to gay-bashing, to the same structures of gender and power. The boys who policed me in fourth grade were enforcing that structure. I'm also a man, and the same structure still hands me advantages I didn't earn. Women blazed this trail. Men arrive second, and our work builds on theirs.

One idea I keep coming back to came from a writer named Utt. A mentor once told him that a man who acts for women without understanding his own reason will act from paternalism. He'll end up harming the people he says he's serving. Utt asks men to follow the leadership of women and non-binary people, and to accept that real liberation costs men status they hold now.

So I've had to name my reason. I know what it costs to be policed into a performance. That cost isn't equal to what women pay under the same system, and I can't let it stand in for their experience. My history gives me a motive to defend everyone's right to be safe. It gives me no claim to speak for the people who are denied that safety.

This gets real when I'm supervising. If someone makes a sexist remark and I stay quiet, my interns learn something from that silence. But if I jump in too hard, I can crowd out the women in the room. My commitment is to name the norm when it shows up. Then I ask the women I work with what they actually need from me, and I don't take over.
