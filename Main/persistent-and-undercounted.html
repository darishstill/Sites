Persistent and Undercounted

Changing the systems behind anti-trans violence

Darish K. Still · BASW Candidate, Millersville University Collaborative research poster · Fall 2026 · with four fellow BASW students

IThe Project

Our group took on transgender oppression. Over six working sessions we had to do two things. First we named a problem we could defend. Then we proposed changes we could actually carry out.

The evidence changed our minds early. We started out calling anti-trans violence “rising.” The national data didn't support that. It did support something just as serious: the violence is persistent, it's disproportionate, and the systems meant to track it badly undercount it. So we rewrote our problem statement to match what we found.

The poster proposes four strategies that work together to count, prevent, and respond:

  1. Count every case. Fix how hate violence gets reported and recorded.
  2. Close the legal gaps. Push for protection, training, and data reporting in Pennsylvania law.
  3. Make public-safety systems accountable. Focus on police training and prison standards.
  4. Fund community prevention and survivor services. Back trans-led programs and the agencies that serve survivors.

Each strategy is built on rights-based, anti-racist, and anti-oppressive lenses. Each one has a roadmap from the first six months through year three.

My part was leading the research on how widespread the violence is and where systems fail to count it. I also wrote Strategy 1, designed the poster, and edited our recorded presentation.

IIIs Anti-Trans Violence Rising?

What the data shows, and what it misses.

When our group started this project, we assumed the answer was yes. I took on the job of checking. What I found was harder to put in a headline, and I think it matters more. A teammate asked us early on to hold the word “rising” until the evidence backed it. She was right.

It's disproportionate. Federal survey data from 2017 to 2020 puts transgender people's rate of violent victimization at 51.5 per 1,000, compared with 20.5 for cisgender people (Truman & Morgan, 2022). That's about two and a half times higher. Fatal violence falls hardest on Black trans women. Of the at least 399 transgender and gender non-conforming people the Human Rights Campaign Foundation has documented as killed since 2013, 70.4% were Black, and 59.4% of cases involved Black trans women (Human Rights Campaign Foundation, 2025).

It's not clearly rising. FBI counts of hate crimes based on gender identity jumped between 2019 and 2022. Since then they've held in roughly the same range, about 470 to 550 incidents a year, and the 2025 count came in below 2023's (Federal Bureau of Investigation, n.d.). The yearly death count tracked by the Human Rights Campaign peaked in 2021 (Human Rights Campaign Foundation, 2022). “Rising” isn't the honest word. “Persistent” is.

And it's undercounted. This was the finding that stopped me. In 2019 the FBI recorded 7,314 hate crime incidents (Federal Bureau of Investigation, 2020). The federal victim survey estimated about 305,390 hate crime victimizations that same year, and found that about 42% of violent hate crimes are never reported to police (Kena & Thompson, 2021). In Pennsylvania, 60% of trans people surveyed said they'd feel uncomfortable asking police for help, and about half of those who'd dealt with police reported some form of mistreatment (National Center for Transgender Equality, 2017).

Then the counting got worse. After a 2025 executive order (Exec. Order No. 14168, 2025), the Justice Department removed the questions that asked survey respondents about their gender identity. That survey can no longer produce a victimization rate for trans people at all (Meyer et al., 2026). Pennsylvania's own hate crime law still leaves gender identity out (Ethnic Intimidation, 18 Pa. Cons. Stat. § 2710). The 2002 law that added it was struck down in court (Marcavage v. Rendell, 2007), and the bill to restore it was tabled in June 2026 (H.B. 1902, 2025).

What complicates the picture. Not all of this violence is strangers acting on hate. One study of fatal violence against Black trans women found that 64.6% had a romantic or sexual relationship with the person who killed them (Halliwell et al., 2025). That points to intimate partner violence, which hate crime law doesn't reach. I also couldn't find a single study showing that an inclusive law by itself reduced violence. What the research does show is that harmful policies are tied to real harm, and that protective policies are tied to less discrimination.

IIIWhy It Matters to My Work

A report is not a count. If a precinct doesn't record a complaint, or a victim expects not to be taken seriously and never files one, the number measures a system's attention more than the violence. Whoever holds the pen decides who gets counted.

In case management, I hold the pen every day. I update service plans every 30 days, and what the chart says becomes what the next provider, funder, and reviewer can see. An intake form with no place for a chosen name or gender identity does to a client what that precinct does to a complaint. It leaves the person uncounted.

So I've committed to a few things. I'll ask every client for the name and pronouns they use, and I'll enter them the same way in every note. I'll refer only to providers who respect them. And because the research documents real harm from police and prisons, I won't treat a call to law enforcement as the default safety plan for a trans client. I'll ask them what safety means to them.

Getting the count right isn't the whole answer. But nothing else works without it.
